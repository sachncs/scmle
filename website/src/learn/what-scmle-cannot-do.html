SCMLE is careful, and it is honest about its limits. This page lists them in plain words, so that you do not trust it for something it does not promise. Every limit here is already stated somewhere in the repository; the sources are linked.

It does not replace your judgement about the problem

SCMLE cannot know:

  • Whether you are predicting the right thing.
  • Whether a column will exist when you use the model. It can suspect a leak from the numbers, never know. unverified means exactly that: nobody checked.
  • Whether the costs of your mistakes are what you wrote down.
  • Whether it is acceptable, fair or legal to use a model for this purpose.
  • Whether the world will stay like your training data.

The tool's job is to make your choices explicit and to stop you when the data disagrees with them. The choices are yours. See Decisions.

It is not a replacement for a data scientist today. Its measured evidence is its own benchmarks, which are not a head-to-head with people.

Statistics: what is guaranteed and what is not

  • A held-out score is an estimate with a band, not a promise. With 216 exam rows, our ROC AUC of 0.885 had a 95 percent band of 0.841 to 0.927. The band assumes new data looks like the data you tested on.
  • Choosing the best of many models is slightly optimistic. The more models tried, the more the winner's practice score flatters it. The exam score is the one to trust (README.md says the same).
  • When no model has any skill, a band can still sit above chance too often, and the cross-validation band did until it was corrected for the choice. All numbers come from benchmarks/statistics/null_selection.py (1,000 rows, a target drawn independently of every column, so every candidate has a true ROC AUC of 0.5, one process on one machine, 200 runs per setting unless a count says otherwise; intervals are Wilson 95 percent). Exam band. Candidates were added by searching hyperparameters of the same three models (3, 9 and 30 finished candidates). The 95 percent band on the winner's exam score contained 0.5 in 193 of 200 runs at each of the three (96.5 percent; 93.0 to 98.3) and lay wholly above 0.5 in 1, 2 and 1 runs. Over 600 runs (three data seeds) it contained 0.5 in 96.7 percent (94.9 to 97.8) at 3 candidates and 95.7 percent (93.7 to 97.0) at 30, and lay above 0.5 in 0.8 percent (0.4 to 1.9) at both. So the number of candidates did not make this band worse in these runs, which is what one expects: the exam rows are opened once, after the winner is frozen, so the choice cannot flatter them. It is not widened for the number of candidates. An earlier measurement of the 3-candidate case on one other 200-run draw found 92 percent (87.3 to 95.4) and 5.0 percent above 0.5; the three draws above did not reproduce it and I do not know why they differ. When the target does depend on two features, the band contained the winner's own ROC AUC (measured on 200,000 fresh rows) in 93.5 percent (89.2 to 96.2) of runs with 3 candidates and 92.5 percent (88.0 to 95.4) with 30 when the true AUC was 0.67, and in 96.0 and 94.5 percent when it was 0.53; the point estimates sit a little under 95 in the strong-signal case, with 95 inside the intervals. Cross-validation band. This one is affected. The winner's mean cross-validation score was above its exam score by 0.021 on average at 3 candidates and 0.034 at 30. Its 95 percent band contained 0.5 in 95.0 percent (93.0 to 96.5; 600 runs) of runs at 3 candidates and 90.3 percent (87.7 to 92.4) at 30, and lay wholly above the baseline's cross-validation score in 4.5 percent (3.1 to 6.5) and 9.7 percent (7.6 to 12.3). The band is now moved down by an estimate of that flattery (selection_optimism in the cv_confidence record): for each fold, pick the candidate with the best mean on the other folds, score it on the held fold, and take the gap to the winner's own mean. With the move, the band contained 0.5 in 95.0 percent (91.0 to 97.3) of 200 runs at 30 candidates (was 89.5 percent on the same runs) and lay above the baseline in 5.0 percent (was 10.5 percent); on the recorded fold scores of 600 runs it was 94.8 percent (92.8 to 96.3) and 4.8 percent. At 3 candidates it is unchanged in the same 200 runs (94.0 percent, 5.0 percent above the baseline), and the width does not change. A Bonferroni widening and a max-over-candidates critical value were tried on the same fold scores and covered 99.8 and 97.8 percent at 30 candidates with bands 2.7 and 1.6 times as wide, so they were not used. What is still not corrected: the estimate assumes the winner is the best mean and ignores tie-breaking and serving limits; it was checked only on these synthetic settings (three model families, ROC AUC, 1,000 rows, stratified folds, null and two signal strengths) and on a least-squares simulation, not on other metrics, tasks, sizes, group or temporal splits; at 3 candidates the cross-validation band still lies above the baseline in about 5 percent of null runs, twice the 2.5 percent a one-sided 95 percent claim would allow; and nested validation is off by default. Read a small lead over the baseline, or a band whose lower end is just above it, as weak evidence.
  • Duplicates can inflate scores. SCMLE warns and keeps copies together in a charter run, but it does not remove them for you.
  • A leakage flag is a suspicion. By default it does not stop the run: it labels the score provisional (block stops). A real strong predictor looks the same to the screen. Only a person who knows when the column is filled in can settle it.
  • No leak is proven absent. A leakage screen that finds nothing means nothing was flagged, not that none exists.
  • Calibrated uncertainty (conformal intervals and sets) holds if new data resembles the calibration data. It is not a guarantee against a changed world.

Claims: confirmed, attested, unverified, and what each is worth

  • confirmed is narrow. It says a finding passed one test on sealed rows with a stated error allowance for the whole list. It says nothing about causes, about other data, or about whether the model is good.
  • The guarantee depends on the charter being honest. A charter written after looking at the data defeats its own purpose. The signature proves possession of the key, not the identity of the person (CLI.md).
  • attested is as good as the person. SCMLE records who vouched. It does not check they were right.
  • unverified is the default for anything a person has not supplied. This includes every cost, every "when is this column known", and every field a language model drafted.
  • Causal claims need a named attestor for every assumption. Without one the tier is unverified. A held-out set alone never confirms a causal effect, and confirmed does not say the assumptions are true. If no identification strategy fits your charter, the claim is not_identified and SCMLE refuses to produce an estimate.
  • Custody is tamper-evident, not adversary-proof. The sealed rows sit in a separate helper process, so ordinary code cannot read them. But a person with the same account on the same machine could read the original file, and the DataFrame you passed in still holds every row. For a hostile setting you need separation: another operating-system user, a service, or write-once storage (SECURITY.md).
  • Newest ledger rows can be cut off unnoticed. The hash chain shows edits, deletions in the middle and reordering, not the removal of the last rows. Keep a copy of the head (ARTIFACTS.md).
  • scmle claims verify proves the files agree with each other. It does not recompute the statistics, and a party who can rewrite every file consistently can pass it.

Certified versus descriptive

A check may stop a run or a release only if its error rate has been measured against planted problems and is on file as a certificate. Everything else is descriptive: shown, never decisive. In practice:

  • Some outputs say descriptive or unverifiable (for example the shift report with no deployment sample). They inform; they do not certify.
  • Stability results are warnings unless a passing certificate for your thresholds exists. Without a charter that asks for it, nothing is withdrawn.
  • The certificate files live in benchmarks/qualification/ in a source checkout. A package installed from pip does not carry them, so detectors there are descriptive until you have the files (ARTIFACTS.md).

The closed loop and escalation

  • The loop's detection-delay gates (H2 and H10) missed their floor in measurement (ARTIFACTS.md).
  • Its rule for when to ask a person has not been compared with a domain owner's own audit, and has not been replayed on recorded incident streams. Do not treat "the loop did not escalate" as "a human would not have".
  • A feedback loop (where the model's own decisions shape the labels you see) cannot be detected without a randomly chosen slice of cases that bypass the model.
  • Retraining, challengers and calibration are Python calls, not commands. The command line cannot, for example, create a signed reply or release a halt.
  • scmle operate runs the loop without a person at the keyboard, and it has only been tried on simulated streams. A person approves every promotion. Because the alarm missed its floors, an alarm asks a person instead of training a challenger. See Autonomous operation.

Serving and security

  • The built-in HTTP server is a minimal reference server: no encryption, no user accounts of its own. It refuses to listen beyond your machine unless token checking is configured, and you must terminate TLS elsewhere (SERVICE_SLO.md).
  • Signatures are shared-secret (HMAC). They prove "someone with this key made this and it has not changed". They are not public-key signatures, a transparency log, or proof of who built a model or that the build machine was honest (ARTIFACTS.md).
  • Model bundles are pickle files. Opening one runs code. That is why signing is mandatory by default.
  • A memory limit is checked every tenth of a second, so a very fast spike can overshoot once before the trial is killed (README.md).
  • Latency and availability objectives are targets measured on a developer machine, not promises under load (SERVICE_SLO.md).

Memory

A run needs several times the table's size in memory, and the multiple grows with the number of numeric columns. These are the only measurements.

  • Setup. An Apple-silicon laptop (12 cores, 19 GB, macOS), Python 3.14.7 free-threaded, pandas 3.0.6, scikit-learn 1.9.1. Project(...).run() with three candidates (dummy, logistic_regression, decision_tree), 3 folds and a seed, on a synthetic table of 30 columns (26 float64, one int64, two text, an int target; 242 bytes per row in memory), at 20,000, 50,000 and 100,000 rows. The figure is the peak physical footprint of the process.
  • Result. With the default cooperative enforcement the peak grew by 3.7 KB per added row, which is 15 times the table, or about 3.7 GB per million rows of this shape. About half of that is the operating system's allocator keeping freed large blocks. With that cache off (MallocLargeCache=0) the slope was 1.9 KB per row (8 times). The allocator-free figure is the one to plan with on systems that return memory at once; it is not known what Linux does.
  • hard enforcement. The main process grew by the same amount (3.8 KB per row, 2.3 with the cache off). Every process it starts imports the libraries again, so the whole tree needed about 0.5 GB more at any size, and grew by 2.7 KB per row (cache off) because a trial process holds the development rows and its own working copies. With several workers each one costs that.
  • Where it goes (cache off, per added row): the caller's table and the project's copy 0.43 KB, the split and the profile 0.75 KB that stays for the whole run, and 0.7 KB of working memory in the step that peaks, a refit, of which 0.6 KB is scikit-learn's median imputer building masked copies of the numeric block.
  • Not measured. Linux, more than 100,000 rows, other model families, hyperparameter search, strategies and ensembles, wide text columns. Tables with fewer or narrower columns have a larger multiple of their own size, because the numeric block is rebuilt as float64. tests/unit/test_memory_growth.py fails if the slope rises above 22 times the table.

Deployment has not been proven end to end

  • The package has never been run against a hosted platform. The release gate and the release registry are tested against recorded answers, scripted runtimes and local processes. How a model reaches a runtime is the user's own adapter.
  • The local demo ran on a developer machine, but Docker was not run: no image was built and no container started (LOCAL.md). The container steps in Start here (build.sh) are untested end to end.
  • Staged traffic (canary) is library code that no release workflow in this repository drives.
  • No production-qualified claim is made. A passing local run is not one.
  • Cost, availability and latency under load on any platform are not claimed.

Scope of what it models

  • Tabular data is the home ground. API_STABILITY.md says which parts of the interface are stable, and the package is in beta: the interface may change between 0.x releases.
  • TabPFN needs its own licence and token. PyTorch and Keras families need an extra install. No language-model client ships; recorded drafts can be replayed, and a replayed draft with no calibrated threshold is refused.
  • Series features use the recent rows stored with the model. A record that predates an entity's stored history gets missing values.
  • Windows: peak-memory reporting counts only the main process, and the free-threaded build is not tested there.
